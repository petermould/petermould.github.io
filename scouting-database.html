Data engineering & multi-criteria analysis

Most football analytics tells you what you already knew. I wanted to look where nobody else was looking.

So I built a rating system for the players nobody had built one for. 3,145 outfield players across five second-tier leagues on two continents, scored with a published method, then checked against an independent professional rating to see whether it actually held up.

The premise

The value is one tier down.

Public football analytics is crowded at the top. There are dozens of models rating Premier League players, and they mostly agree with each other — and with what any regular viewer would tell you anyway. Confirming that the famous players are good isn't scouting.

I wanted to work somewhere with less competition. The leagues one tier down throw off enormous amounts of data and attract almost no analysis: the Championship, 2. Bundesliga, Ligue 2, Serie B and Brasileirão Série B.

That's 100 teams across two continents, covering the 2025-26 season and 2026 for Brazil. I pulled it from three separate providers — FBref, WhoScored and FotMob — and cross-checked everything into a single schema, so a Brazilian second-tier striker can be compared directly against a Championship one.

Method · part one

Letting the data decide what a position is.

FBref's position tags are too blunt to rate players fairly. A plain FW covers both a penalty-box poacher and a wide creative winger, and scoring them on the same criteria punishes each of them for not being the other.

So rather than take the tags at face value, I used K-means clustering to find the real behavioural sub-roles hiding in the statistics. Where FBref's tag was already doing a good job I kept it. Where it wasn't, the clusters replaced it.

Three tags were too broad to rate against a single set of criteria, so K-means split each into two behavioural sub-roles. The other three were already specific enough to keep. Counts are players who met the minutes threshold to be rated.

Method · part two

Not a weighted average. A distance.

Each group is rated with TOPSIS — Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution. It's a published multi-criteria decision method, and I adapted it from a 2024 peer-reviewed paper (Kolbowicz, Nowak & Więckowski) that used the same technique to rate Premier League strikers.

What I like about it is that it's geometric rather than additive. For each position group, the best and worst real values across every criterion define two imaginary reference players — an ideal and a worst case. Every real player then gets scored on how far they sit from each of them. A player rates well not because their stats add up to a big number, but because they sit close to the ideal and far from the worst across 9 to 13 criteria at once.

Shown in two dimensions for clarity. In practice the same calculation runs across 9–13 criteria per position group.

01

Normalise every criterion onto the same scale

Goals per 90 and pass success percentage live on completely different scales, so raw values can't be compared. Each criterion i is rescaled to [0,1] across the position group.

profit criteria   vij = (xij − min xi) ⁄ (max xi − min xi)
cost criteria     vij = (max xi − xij) ⁄ (max xi − min xi)

Cost criteria are the ones where less is better — red cards, errors leading to a shot, goals conceded per 90.

02

Build the two reference players

The best and worst observed value on each criterion define two hypothetical players. Neither exists — they are the corners of the space the real players occupy.

A+ = { max v1, max v2, … max vn }   ideal
A− = { min v1, min v2, … min vn }   worst case
03

Measure Euclidean distance to each

Across all n criteria at once — 9 to 13 depending on the position group — with equal weights, matching the cited paper.

Dj+ = √ Σi=1..n ( vij − Ai+ )²
Dj− = √ Σi=1..n ( vij − Ai− )²
04

Score by relative closeness

The final rating is the share of total distance that sits on the worst-case side. A player identical to the ideal scores 100; one identical to the worst case scores 0.

Cj = Dj− ⁄ ( Dj+ + Dj− ) × 100

Player C scores the most goals and finishes bottom. That is the point of the method: C is the best on one criterion and the worst on the other two, so geometrically he sits far from the ideal. Player A leads on nothing at all and still rates second, because he is never far from the ideal on anything. A weighted average would have ranked C first.

I kept the weights equal within each group, matching the paper's methodology rather than inventing my own weighting that I'd have no way to defend.

Validation

Checked against a rating system I did not build.

A rating system that's never been tested against anything external is just an opinion with decimal places. So I checked mine against WhoScored's independent professional ratings across 488 matched players.

It came back at a Spearman correlation of ρ = 0.621 (p < 0.001), which sits inside the 0.42–0.77 range the original paper reported for the same validation. Enough agreement to show the method is picking up something real — and nowhere near perfect agreement, which is where it got interesting.

The finding

The two systems disagree in one direction, and the direction is explainable.

Forwards score up to 91 ranks higher in my system. Midfielders score up to 38 ranks higher on WhoScored. That's not noise — it's a clean split by position.

It comes down to what each one is measuring. WhoScored rates every individual match and averages across the season, which rewards turning up consistently and so favours midfielders. Mine rates full-season totals, which rewards cumulative output and so favours forwards. Neither is wrong. The disagreement is the finding — and it tells you which system to reach for depending on whether you're scouting for reliability or for output.

He comes out top among central forwards across the whole dataset, and it holds up when you look underneath. He's a Brazilian second-tier striker who'd never turn up on a Big-5-focused scout's radar — which is exactly the gap I built this to close.

One caveat worth saying out loud: scores are worked out within position groups, so a goalkeeper's 75.3 and a forward's 67.6 aren't directly comparable — each one measures distance from the ideal for that role. The Best XI above takes the leader of each group; it isn't the whole dataset ranked on one axis.

Debugging log

Four bugs that changed how I build pipelines.

Every one of these produced output that looked completely fine. None of them threw an error anywhere near the point where the damage was actually done.

Identity

The impossible duplicate player. One player showed up sixteen times, with slightly different stats in each row. Two things turned out to be happening at once: two genuinely different players shared a name and a club — both called Marvin Schulz, aged 21 and 30, at the same German side — and repeated merges had Cartesian-multiplied rows on top of that coincidence. I rebuilt the deduplication around a proper identity key of name, club, league, age and birth year, which collapsed the accidental duplicates and kept both real players.

Parsing

An age column that was 100% missing. One league came back with no ages at all. Total absence is suspicious in a way that partial absence isn't — real missing data is rarely complete. The culprit was a locale-specific format, 29-236, meaning 29 years and 236 days. The integer parser turned it into NaN without complaining. A string split before the numeric conversion sorted it.

Statistics

A finding that turned out to be an artefact. A sensitivity analysis showed pass success percentage driving rating changes five times bigger than any other stat. For a while I thought I'd found something. I hadn't. Pass success only occupies a narrow real-world range, roughly 70–90%, so a 10% relative improvement pushed a player across nearly half of it, while the same 10% on a rate-based stat barely moved them. Switching percentage-based criteria to a fixed percentage-point increase made the comparison fair again.

Architecture

A pipeline that quietly erased its own work. Four per-league scripts run one after another, each wiping the previous league's merged results — because every script's cleanup step operated on the whole dataset instead of just its own league. I only caught it because one league had gone blank after a run that reported success. Rather than patch the symptom, I rewrote the merge as a single pass that processes every league once and saves once, which killed the whole category of bug.

Getting the data
